                                          Grace Period for Model Codes

As you  know,  the  2024  I-Codes,  2024  National  Standard  Plumbing  Code,  and  2023  NFPA  70  were  adopted  as  of
August 17,  2026.  With  this  adoption  comes  a six-month  grace  period  for the  previous  model  code.  This  means  that
starting from August 17, 2026, applicants may submit a complete permit application, including all prior approvals, to be
reviewed under  the  code  in  force  immediately  preceding  the  subcode  revision.  Provided  the  application  is  complete,
the construction  official  and  applicable  subcode  officials  should  perform  the  plan  review  and  issue  construction
permit(s) based  on  the  code in  force  immediately  prior  to the  operative date of  the  subcode  revision.  The  last  day  to
submit an application using the previous codes is February 17, 2027.

Therefore, if a complete permit application was submitted on or between August 17, 2026, and February 17, 2027, the
project would  be  permitted  to be  reviewed  under  the  old  codes  (2021/2020).  The  application  should  explicitly  state
which codes  were  utilized  for  the  design  of  the  project. If  the code  edition  is  not  explicitly  stated,  officials  should  ask
which edition was used.

For completeness,  the  application  must  include  the  items  listed  at  N.J.A.C.  5:23-2.15,  Construction  permits  —
application. Please note that N.J.A.C.  5:23-2.15(a) includes  all elements  that are required in order to submit  a permit
application; N.J.A.C. 5:23-2.15(b) provides for further requirements on an application for a permit when the information
becomes available,  no  later  than  the  commencement  of  work.  This  means  that  any  of  the  information  listed  in  (b)
would not be required in order to consider a permit application complete.

Also note that prototype plan releases based on the previously adopted edition of the codes remain valid until the end
of the grace period. February     17, 2027, would thus   be the last day to apply for a permit with the released    prototype.

                                          Revised UCC Technical Forms

Technical  Assistants,  Subcode  Officials,              and  Construction  Officials  should  be  aware  that  the   department,  in
consultation  with  the  applicable  subcode  committees,  revised  subcode  technical  section  forms  in  December  2025
(note: the revision date appears in the bottom              -left corner after the U      niform Construction       Code (UCC) form number).

receptacles, and switches into one item, “Devices,” to save space on the form. New items such as EV Chargers                                        and
PV Systems, which have become commonplace, have been added.

Additionally,  all  forms  listed  on  a  municipality's  website  for  applicants  to  download  or  print  should  be  the  current
version (12/25).

Most importantly, a municipality cannot reject a permit application using the re                    vised forms and ask applicants to submit
previous versions. Technical  assistants and  officials  are reminded that they may use  only the UCC  forms found in
N.J.A.C. 5:23-4.5(b). As per N.J.A.C. 5:23            -4.5(b)4, “No forms other than those established by the Commissioner shall
be  required  of  the  public  in  connection  with  the  administration  and  enforcement  of  the  State  Uniform  Construction
Code. The municipal enforcing agency may use additional forms for its own internal processing and recordkeeping.”

                          Garden State Balcony Solar Act and the UCC

This article is to provide additional clarification on the Uniform Construction Code (UCC) impacts of Senate Bill 2368,
also known as the Garden State Balcony Solar Act, which was signed into law on September 1st, 2026, as P.L.2026,
c.82.

The law defines a “portable solar generation device” as a moveable photovoltaic generation device and associated
equipment:  (1)  has  a  maximum  power  output  of  not  more  than  1,200  watts;  (2)  is  designed  to  be  connected  to  a
building’s electrical system through a standard 120-volt alternating current outlet; (3) is located on the customer’s side
of the electrical meter and is intended primarily to offset part of the customer’s electricity consumption; (4) is listed or
certified in accordance with Underwriters Laboratories Standard No. 3700, the “Outline of Investigation for Interactive
Plug-in Photovoltaic Equipment and Systems,” or a comparable standard, or a combination of standards that together
address plug-in photovoltaic safety and grid-parallel by a nationally recognized testing laboratory; and (5) includes a
device or feature that prevents the portable solar generation device from exporting power to the electrical distribution
system during a power outage.

The law states that the plug-in device must comply with the applicable installation provisions of the product listing and,
when necessary, the applicable installation provisions of the electrical subcode, the National Electrical Code/NFPA 70.
Because  the  law  covers  the  listing/labeling  portion  of  the  electrical  subcode,  the  plug-in  device  would  be  covered
under the code. The law further notes that devices that have a maximum power output to the receptacle outlet of 400
watts  or  less  shall  be  exempt  from  any  provision  in  the  product  listing,  National  Electrical  Code,  or  State  Uniform
Construction Code that would require alterations to the building’s premises, wiring, or electrical panels.

Furthermore, the law notes that no municipality shall require a permit, application, fee, license, or other approval for
the placement or use of a portable solar generation device. The provisions of this subsection shall not be construed to
exempt  any  building  modification,  electrical  work,  or  other  alteration  from  the  applicable  provisions  of  the  National
Electrical Code, the State Uniform Construction Code adopted pursuant to the “State Uniform Construction Code Act,”
P.L.1975, c.217 (C.52:27D-119 et seq.), or any
municipal permit or inspection requirements.

Accordingly, the Department maintains that the language of the law prohibits requiring a permit for the placement or
use of the portable solar generation device; however, it does not mean a permit is not required for any associated
building  modification.  With  this  being  the  case,  if  a  building  modification  is  undertaken  for  the  device,  such  as  the
relocation or addition of an electrical receptacle on a balcony, any UCC permit that would normally be taken out for
such work would still be required.

For a more in-depth view of the law, please see the link to P.L.2026, c.82 below.

https://pub.njleg.state.nj.us/Bills/2026/S2500/2368_R3.PDF

                                         Spec Sheets for Replacements

Minor  work  requires  notification  to  the  local  enforcement  office,  preferably  in  writing,  before  you  begin  the  work  or
replacement. So, you notify the local and then replace the boiler, water heater, or HVAC unit, etc.; then submit your
application, either in person or electronically, and wait for notification to pay for your permit. But instead, the Township
tells  you  they  need  two  copies  of  the  replacement  unit  specs  that  were  missing  during                  plan  review.  Plan  review?!
Minor work does not include plan review, and what exactly are you looking for in the specs? BTU load? That is on the
chimney verification form. Clearances? How are you going to verify clearances from your code official’s office? Also,
per the 2021 International Residential Code NJ Edition Section G2408.1 and the 2021 International Fuel Gas Code
Section 305.1, “Manufacturer’s installation instructions shall be available on the job site at the time of inspection,” so
any clearance questions would be addressed on site.

All code enforcement offices are responsible for maintaining files and materials for prescribed amounts of time, per
N.J.A.C.  5:23-2.16A,  and  record  retention  costs  a  lot  of  money.  So  now  you  have  to  store  these  unnecessary
documents for a minimum of ten years, in most cases, causing the Township or office to allocate funds for these specs
that are required to be on site for the inspection. But hey, it’s your money! Spend or waste it as you see fit, I guess.

                         Significant Changes to the 2024 IBC and IRC

The  purpose  of  this  article  is  to  familiarize  code  officials,  design  professionals,  contractors,  and  homeowners  with
many of the important changes in the 2024 International Building Code                       (IBC)   and the 2024 International Residential
Code (IRC). This article discusses only a portion of the code changes. When using the IBC and IRC, note that the
solid  vertical  lines  in  the  margins  indicate  a  technical  change  from  the  requirements  of  the  2021  edition.  Deletion
indicators in the form of an arrow are provided in the margin where an entire section, paragraph, exception, or table
has been deleted, or an item in a list of items or a row of a table has been deleted. A single asterisk [*] placed in the
margin indicates that text or a table has been relocated within the code. A double asterisk [**] placed in the margin
indicates that the text or table immediately following has been relocated there from elsewhere in the code. For those
using the digital format, the blue text indicates a technical change from the requirements of the 2021 edition and red
text indicates NJ amendments.

Significant Changes to the 2024 IBC:

Definitions

     •    High-Rise Building: This definition now includes occupiable roofs in the parameters to determine whether a
          building is considered a high-rise. The language in this definition references a “floor that is more than 75 feet
          above the  lowest level  of fire department vehicle access.” Occupants and combustible furnishings on roofs
          make  ground-based  firefighting  more  difficult  and  limit  firefighters'  ability  to  perform  rescue  operations.  As
          such,  their  inclusion  in  the  determination  of  a  high-rise  provides  additional  safety  provisions  in  these
          circumstances.
     •    Incapable  of      Self-Preservation:        Describes  persons  who,  because  of  age,  physical  limitations,  mental
          limitations,  chemical  dependency,  or  medical  treatment, cannot  respond  as  an  individual  to an emergency.
          The movement of all persons (occupants, residents and staff) to an exit in more than 13 minutes.
     •    Limited    Verbal  or     Physical     Assistance:       Describes  persons  who,  because  of  age,  physical  limitations,
          cognitive  limitations,  treatment  or  chemical  dependency,  may  not  independently  recognize,  respond  or
          evacuate  without  limited  verbal  or  physical  assistance  during  an  emergency.  Limited  verbal  assistance
          includes  prompting,  giving  and  repeating  instructions.  Limited  physical  assistance  includes  assistance  with
          transfers  to  walking  aids  or  mobility  devices  and  assistance  with  egress.  The  movement  of  all  persons
          (occupants, residents, and staff) to an exit  in more than 3 minutes, but not more than 13  minutes. NJ has
          deleted  “Incapable  of  Self-Preservation"  and  instead  used  the  definitions  “Evacuation,  Impractical”  and
          “Evacuation, Slow.” As the model codes have evolved over the years, these terms have caused considerable
          confusion among code users. However, the deleted definitions had an assigned time limit. This time limit will
          now be added to the end of the definitions above, as shown.
     •    Occupiable Roof: The new term “occupiable roof” replaces the former “occupied roof” designation to clarify
          that  the  applicable  provisions  are  triggered  by  the  roof's  intended  use,  rather  than  by  whether  the  roof  is
          actually  occupied  at  a  given  time.  The  term  “occupiable”  does  not  apply  to  roofs  accessed  solely  for
          maintenance,  including  maintenance  of  the  building  or  rooftop                 equipment  such  as  mechanical  systems.
          Introduced in the 2024 edition, this definition aligns with the code’s existing definition of “occupiable space.”
          Therefore,  a  roof  designed  for  human  occupancy  is  considered  “occupiable”  and  subject  to  the  applicable
          code requirements.
     •    Temporary Event: A single use during the service life of a public-occupancy temporary structure at a given
          location that includes its installation, inspection, use and occupancy, and dismantling.

     •    Temporary Structure: Any building or structure erected for a period of 180 days or less to support temporary
          events.  Temporary  structures  include  a  range  of  structure  types  (public-occupancy  temporary  structures,
          temporary  special  event  structures,  tents,  umbrellas  and  other  membrane  structures,  relocatable  buildings,
          temporary  bleachers,  etc.)  for  a  range  of  purposes  (storage,  equipment  protection,  dining,  workspace,
          assembly, etc.).

          These new definitions clarify requirements in the new Section 3103, which sets forth requirements for public-
          occupancy  temporary  structures.  The  general  public  or  public  events  use  these  structures,  and  occupants
          expect  similar  levels  of  reliability  and  safety  as  offered  by  permanent  construction.  These  terms  apply  to
          structures in service for no longer than one year and include adjustments that reduce certain environmental
          load requirements based on their temporary nature while recognizing the consistent level of risk.

Sections, Tables, and Appendices

     •    306.2  Moderate-Hazard           Factory     Industrial,  Group  F-1.         Due  to  the  anticipated  hazards,  several  uses
          associated with the manufacture of lithium-ion and lithium-metal batteries have been added to the list of Group
          F-1  occupancies.  In  addition,  facilities  used  for  the  manufacture  or  packaging  of  alcoholic  beverages  are
          classified as Group F-1, rather than Group F-2, only when the beverages have an alcohol content greater than
          20 percent. Beverages up to and including 20 percent will be classified as                       Group F-2. A similar change has
          been added to Group S-1 and S-2 when storing the above contents.
     •    503.1.4.1  Enclosures         Over    Occupiable       Roof    Areas.     New  Exception  2  allows  other  rooftop  structures,
          including barriers, guards, parapets, bulkheads, and mechanical equipment screens, that may be located on
          the roof but are not specifically exempted by Exception 1. This allowance applies where the building qualifies
          as  a  high-rise  building.  It's  recognized  that  the  roof  deck  would  be  located  at  a  height  greater  than  that
          available for fire department access from the building’s exterior.
     •    Table 903.2.13 Additional Required Protection Systems. Table 903.2.13, along with the correlated section,
          has been deleted. This amendment was added to the IBC with the 2000 adoption of the code to address the
          increased area allowance for these occupancies. To date, there have been limited issues with the increased
          area  allowances  for  these  occupancies;  therefore,  the  Department  is  deleting  these  added  fire  sprinkler

                                     Adult Changing Stations: 2024 IBC

The 2024 International Building Code (IBC) introduces a new accessibility requirement that facility owners and code
officials should be aware of: “adult changing stations.” While changing tables for infants and toddlers have become
standard in many public restrooms, adults with disabilities who require assistance have had far fewer dignifi                          ed options,
which are often limited to a restroom floor. Section 1110.4, Adult changing stations, addresses this gap by establishing
the scoping requirements for where adult changing stations are required.

Where Required

According to Section 1110.4 of the 2024 IBC, not fewer than one adult changing station must now be provided in four
categories of occupancies:

     1.   Assembly  and  mercantile             occupancies  where  family/assisted-use  toilet  or  bathing  rooms  are  already
          required under Section 1110.2.1;
     2.   Group B occupancies offering education above the 12th grade, where an aggregate of 12 or more male and
          female water closets serve classrooms and lecture halls;
     3.   Group E occupancies, where an assembly-purpose room or space requires an aggregate of six or more male
          and female water closets; and
     4.   Highway rest stops and service plazas. More specifically, facilities accessible only from the highway, not
          private businesses located nearby.

Location and Design Requirements

     •    Adult  changing  stations  must  be  located  in  toilet  rooms               with  only  one  water  closet  and  one  lavatory
          (family/assisted-use rooms qualify).
     •    The accessible route cannot require passing through a security checkpoint.
     •    Travel distance is limited to 2,000 feet, and the station must be within two stories above or below the occupied
          story, which is a longer allowance than the family/assisted-use toilet room standard, to limit how many large
          stations must be installed.
     •    Where voluntarily provided (even if not required), stations must still meet these same accessibility stan                       dards.

                                                2024 IRC Door Closers

This article explains that, under the 2024 International Residential Code (IRC), openings between a dwelling unit and
an attached garage must be self         -latching and equipped with a self        -closing or automatic-closing device, as required by
Section R302.5.1, Opening Protection.

For background, this requirement was first established in the 2012 IRC, which New Jersey did not adopt. When New
Jersey adopted the 2015 IRC, Section R302.5.1 was amended to delete the portion of the last sentence that required
the opening to be “equipped with a self-closing device.” At the time, the Department determined that installing self-
closing devices was prohibitively expensive and that the benefits did not outweigh the cost of compliance.

However,  Section  R302.5.1  does  not  specify  a  testing  standard  or  particular  type  of  closing  device.  During  the
Department’s review, we found that spring hinges can provide an affordable option where a surface                          -mounted closer is
not used. In addition, the 2024 IRC added requirements for self-closing doors serving garages or rooms containing
energy storage systems due to the increased fire hazards associated with these systems.

Recognizing that garages, even those without energy storage systems, are increasingly being used to house electric
vehicles and electric bicycles, the Department determined that applying the self-closing requirement consistently to all
attached garages provides an appropriate level of protection and is a reasonable and affordable requirement for new
construction.

Inspectors  are  only  required  to  verify  that  the  door  closes  and  latches  without  intervention.  There  are  no  specific
closing-speed or pounds-of-force requirements to enforce.

So, yes, you can say that the Department is officially             closing the door on its previous amendment found in Section
5:23-3.21(c)3ix.

                                              2024 IBC and ASCE 7-22

One of the most significant changes to the 2024 International Building Code (IBC) is the new corresponding ASCE 7-
22  reference  standard.      For  background,  earlier  editions  of  ASCE  7  used  ground  snow  load  maps  based  on  older
National  Weather  Service  data  collected  from  1952–1992.  These  maps  remained  largely  unchanged  from  1995
through 2016 and included numerous “Case Study” areas where insufficient data required engineers to perform their
own snow-load analyses. Note that the 2021 IBC referenced ASCE 7-16.

The  2024  IBC  adopts  the  ASCE         7-22  snow  load  maps  and  references  ASCE  7-22  for  snow-load  design.  The  free
ASCE Hazard Tool, which is also referenced in Bulletin 19-1, can be used to determine the applicable ground snow
load based on location and risk category.

ASCE  7-22  updates  the  snow  load  provisions  using  additional  snow  data,  updated  calculation  methods,  strength                      -
design-based values, and improved mapping. These updates provide more consistent ground snow loads, reduce the
number  of  Case  Study  areas,  particularly  in  mountainous  regi           ons,  and  help  address  differences  in  design  values
across state boundaries.

It is worth noting that the updated snow loads may be higher in some areas. The ASCE Hazard Tool can also be used
to determine wind speeds, although the changes to wind speeds are generally less significant.

                  Generators Located Outdoors: Residential Setbacks

All the requirements for this article can be found in National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) 37                       -2018. However, I
believe  it’s  important  to  find  the  code  path  from  each  applicable  subcode  to  the  respective  reference  standard
because, in this case, you won’t find a direct link to NFPA 37-2018 in the 2021 International Residential Code (IRC).

The path from the IRC to NFPA 37-2018 is challenging, but nevertheless, the path does exist. Neither the 2021 IRC
mechanical system requirements of chapters 12-24, nor the IRC fuel gas requirements of chapter 24 contain specific
requirements for generators, but this is not a dead end. Both the mechanical section at Section M1301.1 and the fuel
gas  section  at  Section  G2401.1  provide  similar  blanket  statements  that  appliances,  equipment,  systems,  etc.,  not
specifically covered by the respective chapters, shall comply with the applicable provisions of the 2021 International
Fuel Gas Code (IFGC) and/or the 2021 International Mechanical Code (IMC). IFGC Section 616 and IMC Section 915
both reference NFPA 37-2018 for engine- and gas turbine-powered equipment.

Now on to the requirements of NFPA 37-2018: “The Standard for the Installation and Use of Stationary Engines and
Gas Turbines:  NFPA 37-2018 at Section 4.1.4.1 requires that an engine and its housing (enclosure) be located at
least  5  feet  from  openings  and  at  least  5  feet  from  structures  with  combustible  walls.  Section  4.1.4.1  does  not
distinguish between operable and fixed windows/openings. Therefore, a generator must be kept at least 5 feet from all
openings regardless of the opening’s operability. The only exception to this                    rule is found at Section 4.1.4.1.1. This
section permits clearances of less than 5ft if all portions of a structure (including openings) that are closer than 5 feet
to the engine enclosure have a fire resistance rating of at least 1hr. Finally, the reduction permitted at Section 4.1.4.1.2
for full-scale fire tests applies to combustible structures only and does not apply to openings.

                         Outdoor outlets, AC units, Ground Fault Protection,
                                                    and the Rehab Subcode

Since the adoption of the 2023 Nat           ional Electrical Code (NFPA 70), as adopted under N.J.A.C. 5:23                     -3.16, on August
17, 2026, both the Office of Regulatory Affairs and the Code Assistance Unit have received countless calls regarding
enforcement of Article 210.8(F), Outdoor outlets.             This particularly pertains to Exception No. 2 and its expiration, which
required GFCI protection for the outlet supplying listed HVAC equipment. This exception was added to the 2020 NEC,
carried over to the 2023 NEC, and then expired on September 1, 2026. NFPA did                           not extend this exception, at least at
the time this article was printed.

NEC Article 210.8 requires that a listed Class A GFCI shall provide protection in accordance with 210.8(A) through (F).
The GFCI shall be  installed  in  a readily accessible  location. This charging text verifies that a Class A ground fault
protection  device  SHALL  be  provided  for  ALL  receptacle  outlets,  outlets,  or  branch  circuits  as  necessary  in
accordance with 210.8(A) through (F). For this Communicator, we are focused on Article 210.                              8(F), Exception No. 2.
For context, the entire section states:

          (F) Outdoor Outlets.

          For  dwellings,  all  outdoor  outlets,  other  than  those  covered  in  210.8(A),  Exception  No.  1,  including  outlets
          installed  in  the  following  locations,  and  supplied  by  single        -phase  branch  circuits  rated  150  volts  or  less  to
          ground, 50 amperes or less, shall be provided with GFCI protection:

                    (1) Garages that have floors located at or below grade level
                    (2) Accessory buildings
                    (3) Boathouses

          If equipment supplied by an outlet c          overed under the requirements of this section is replaced, the outlet shall
          be supplied with GFCI protection.

          Exception  No.  1:       GFCI  protection  shall  not  be  required  on  lighting  outlets  other  than  those  covered  in
          210.8(C).

          Exception No. 2: GFCI protection shall not be required for listed HVAC equipment. This exception shall
          expire September 1, 2026.

Let’s first dissect the section as applied to listed            HVAC equipment installed outdoors: when the equipment’s outlet is
supplied by a single      -phase branch circuit rated 150 volts or less to ground and 50 amps or less, it shall be provided
with  GFCI  protection.  Article  210.18,  in  short,  states  the  rating  of  a  branch  circuit  is  determined  by  the  size  of  the
overcurrent protection device, not the equipment rating nor the conductor size. As stated in the charging text of Article
210.8, a listed Class A ground fault protection device is required;                 no other Class GFCI meets the requirement.

This  article  only  applies  to  newly  installed,  not  replaced  outdoor  HVAC  equipment  in  either  new  dwellings,  new
additions to dwellings, or new never          -existing equipment installed in existing dwellings.

It  seems  possible  subcode  officials  and  inspectors  develop  amnesia  whenever  a  new  adoption  to  the  code  comes
along, especially when applied to rehab projects. Before you all get upset, let me explain. N.J.A.C 5:23                             -6.1(a)1 states:

          The  provisions  of  the  other  subcodes  of  the  Uniform  Co              nstruction  Code  do  not  apply  to  work  in  existing
          buildings, changing the use of an existing building or work in an existing building related to an addition                             unless
          the provisions of this subcode specifically reference them and make them applicable                           .

What that means is Subchapter 6 is the starting point for all work in existing buildings, and Subchapter 3 subcodes do
not apply unless directed “specifically by reference” in Subchapter 6, aka Rehab. Inspectors cannot simply open the
NEC and point to Article 210         .8(F) on an HVAC replacement to tell the applicant they must comply.

So, if rehab applies and is our compliance starting point, how can we cite Article 210.8(F) for new installations in an
existing dwelling? Easy: when using the rehab subcode, the first thing to determine is the scope of work and which
rehab category the work falls under, as defined in N.J.A.C. 5:23                     -6.3 Definitions. In this case, “the installation of any
additional equipment or fixtures” determines that newly installed HVAC equipment is an                            alteration by definition.

     •    N.J.A.C.  5:23-6.1(d)4  Alteration  work  must  also  comply  with  materials  and  methods  that  are  set  forth  in
          N.J.A.C. 5:23-6.8.

     •    N.J.A.C.  5:23-6.1(d)7  Certain  alterations  create  what  are  defined  to  be  new  building  elements.  There  is  a
          specific listing of those items that are to be treated as newly created building elements, which can be found in
          N.J.A.C. 5:23-6.9. The elements on that list are required to conform to certain specific sections of the other
          subcodes of the Uniform Construction Code. The sections with which each newly constructed element must
          comply are listed in N.J.A.C. 5:23-6.9. Any alteration which creates one of the elements listed in that section
          must comply with the specific requirements listed in N.J.A.C. 5:23-6.9.

     •    N.J.A.C.     5:23-6.9(c)2      Newly   installed   (not   replacing   an   existing   device)   heating,   air   conditioning   or
          refrigeration equipment likely to require examination, adjustment, servicing or maintenance shall conform with
          the  requirements  at  N.J.A.C.  5:23-6.8,  Materials  and  methods, and,  in  addition,  shall  conform  with  Section
          210.63(A)  Heating,  Air  Conditioning,  and  Refrigeration  Equipment  Outlet  and,  if  newly  installed  in  an  attic,
          underfloor space, utility room or basement, 210.70 Lighting Outlets Required, as applicable, of the electrical
          subcode.

Now that we have a path of enforcement, we can look at N.J.A.C. 5:23-6.8, Methods and materials, and can determine
that Article 210.8(F) is not deleted and is part of our scope of work, as we are installing a new, never-before-installed
outlet for outdoor HVAC equipment therefore, Class A ground fault protection shall be required.

But  wait...  the  methods  and  materials  section  doesn’t  delete  Article  210.8(F),  and  N.J.A.C.  5:23-6.1(c)4  also  says
renovation work must comply with N.J.A.C. 5:23-6.8, Methods and Materials, so I can still cite Article 210.8(F) for the
replacement of an outdoor HVAC unit! How about that, Regulatory Affairs and Code Assistance? You are wrong; I win
this time!

             Tyco Fire Sprinklers Recall: Sprinklers that Can Fail in a Fire

Although identifying buildings affected by the recall will fall mostly within the purview of fire officials                         licensed through
the Division of Fire Safety (DFS), fire protection subcode officials should still be aware of this recall. Replacing these
sprinklers  qualifies  as  renovation  work  as  defined  at  N.J.A.C.  5:23              -6.3;  this  renovation  work  would  be  considered
ordinary maintenance under N.J.A.C. 5:23-2.7 and would therefore not require a permit. However, a DFS-certified fire
protection contractor is still required to perform the sprinkler work. Additionally, as always, the fire protection subcode
official must notify the fire official of any planned sprinkler work that will impair the sprinkler system for an extended
period so the fire official can implement a fire watch before the impairment.

More about the recall as found on the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission website:

          “Washington,  D.C.        – The  U.S.  Consumer  Product  Safety  Commission  (CPSC)  and  Tyco  Fire  Products  LP
          (Tyco Fire) are alerting the public again about serious, potentially fatal fire                  -safety hazards posed by the failure
          to  replace  previously       recalled  Central  Sprinkler  fire  sprinkler  heads  that  may  still  be  installed  in  homes,
          residential facilities, and commercial buildings across the United States.

The affected sprinklers, manufactured from the  mid-1970s to June 2001, were originally recalled in July 2001. The
recall was subsequently modified in May 2003 and closed on August 31, 2007, after millions of sprinkler heads were
replaced. Minerals, salts, and other contaminants in water can degrade the rubber O                           -ring seals, which may cause the
sprinkler  heads  to  fail  to  activate  during  a  fire.  Despite  a  multi-year  remediation  effort  and  National  Fire  Protection
Association standards requiring annual, 5-year, and 20-year inspections, testing, and maintenance of fire suppression
systems, many unremediated recalled sprinklers may remain in service, potentially posing hazards to consumers.

On  July  13,  2025,  a  fire  occurred  at  an  assisted  living  facility  in  Fall  River,  MA.  The  fire  claimed  the  lives  of  ten
residents  and  injured  dozens  more.  The  facility  had  not  replaced  dozens  of  recalled  sprinkler  heads,  despite
identification  during  a  prior  fire-safety  inspection.  The  fire  underscores  the  need  to  remind  facilities  of  the  dangers
posed by unremediated sprinklers and encourage remediation wherever recalled units are still in use.
